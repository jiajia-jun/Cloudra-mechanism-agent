Abstract
Autonomous reaction-mechanism exploration requires proposing reaction steps, preparing three-dimensional structures, and determining
which reactant and product a computed saddle point connects. Large language model (LLM) agents can misjudge spatial relationships and
confuse a computed structure with the species or step they intended to study.
We present Cloudra, a multi-agent framework that constrains mechanistic output through a Delivery DAG of chemical objects. Species
nodes carry geometries and bonding topology; elementary-step edges carry transition states and reaction connections between species.
Cheminformatics algorithms establish molecular identities and atom correspondences. Deterministic routines measure and transform struc-
tures to prepare candidate geometries. Frequency and IRC calculations determine the stationary points found and the species they connect.
Agents use these objects to test proposed steps, compare pathways, and revise the mechanism.
CloudraBench evaluates the framework’s end-to-end mechanism-calculation capability across 50 tasks covering elementary reactions, multi-
step mechanisms, stereochemical competition, and computational method limitations. Across 200 runs under four workflow configurations,
the primary Guided workflow achieves a mean composite score of 0.805, compared with 0.584 for the same-model Standard baseline. On
Hard-tier tasks, the Guided mean increases from 0.689 under Standard to 0.958. Under Guided, the self-consistency component reaches its
maximum score in 24 of the 40 non-Impossible tasks, compared with seven under Standard. Behavioral analyses examine how the agents
use bond and dihedral measurements, optimized structures, and IRC endpoints to revise local mechanistic assignments.
1 Introduction
1.1 Physical requirements for autonomous reaction
mechanism research
Reaction-mechanism validation verifies that molecular ge-
ometries, reaction pathways, and quantum-chemical results
correspond to the declared species and elementary steps.
Frequency analysis identifies minima and first-order saddles,
and intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) calculations identify
the species connected by each saddle. The computed end-
points determine the saddle’s mechanistic assignment.[ 1, 2]
Automated reaction-network exploration organizes calcu-
lations through chemical objects. Within SCINE, Chemoton
2.0 groups structures into compounds and elementary steps
into reactions, and validates optimized saddles through IRC
calculations, molecular-graph comparisons, and product
optimization.[3] AutoRXN combines autonomous reaction
exploration with density-functional calculations, coupled-
cluster energy refinement, and automated multireference
diagnostics.[4]
Computational chemistry agents automate several parts of
this work. El Agente Q [ 5], Aitomia [ 6], and Gráfico [ 7]
orchestrate quantum-chemical calculations and typed work-
flows. Estructural [ 8] and ARCHE [ 9] provide tools for
molecular fragment editing, atom mapping, and transition-
state geometry preparation; Quntur [ 10] addresses reaction
kinetics and transition-state research. Extending these capa-
bilities to autonomous mechanism exploration requires reli-
able geometric operations and physical interpretation of the
species and pathways obtained.
Aligning stereochemical orientations, assembling reac-
tant fragments, and preparing pathway endpoints require
three-dimensional geometric reasoning. Language models
can make errors when they infer spatial relationships or di-
rectly manipulate Cartesian coordinates. Quntur [10], for ex-
ample, reports the need for human assistance with endpoint
alignment in complex pathway and transition-state prepara-
tion. In Cloudra, an agent can ask the program to rotate
the atoms on one side of a bond, measure the resulting di-
hedral angle, and compare the new geometry with another
transition-state guess.
1.2 Cloudra: autonomous mechanism exploration
through chemical objects
Tool-oriented workflow graphs use calculation calls as nodes
and execution dependencies as edges. Cloudra is a multi-
agent system for small-molecule mechanism exploration that
represents the delivered reaction mechanism through species
and elementary-step objects in a Delivery DAG. Agents ex-
press candidate species and elementary steps in this graph,
choose geometric operations and subsequent calculations
from the associated structures, energies, and pathway results,
and deliver the resulting mechanism through the same ob-
jects.
Figure 1 summarizes a structured literature sample of 85
chemical AI agent systems: 39 concern materials or surfaces,
23 concern finite molecules or complexes, 12 concern soft
matter or biomolecules, and 11 cover multiple categories of
objects. Figures 2 and 3 show the documented tasks in the
finite-molecule/complex category and compare Cloudra’s
capabilities with those of the existing systems. The accom-
panying literature materials contain the complete matrix and
the sources for each entry.
Cloudra uses deterministic routines to calculate and mod-
ify molecular coordinates. MCS matching selects molecu-
lar fragments; atom mapping matches reactant and product
atoms and identifies atom pairs whose bonds change. The
program measures bond lengths, bond angles, dihedral an-
gles, and stereochemistry, and rotates or translates selected
atoms to prepare candidate structures.

1.3 Contributions
Cloudra’s central design connects LLM research decisions
and mechanism delivery through chemical objects. The De-
livery DAG specifies the species and steps under investiga-
tion and the calculation results required to support them.
Molecular topology and stereochemistry identify species;
atom mappings track atoms across structures. Geometry op-
erations construct candidates, and frequency and IRC cal-
culations assign the resulting stationary points and reaction
connections. The agents use these objects to extend and re-
vise the mechanism as the study progresses.
The geometric procedures include MCS-based fragment
selection, graph traversal to select the atoms that move, and
transition-state fragment assembly. They allow the agents to
rotate selected groups, adjust fragment positions, measure
the resulting structures, and prepare new guesses after an
unsuccessful transition-state search.
We evaluate the framework’s end-to-end mechanism-
calculation capability using CloudraBench, which measures
graph coverage, physical self-consistency, and task-defined
target accuracy from native computational outputs. Its 50
tasks cover elementary and multistep reactions, stereochem-

2 System Design and Methods
2.1 System Overview and Division of Responsibilities
Cloudra investigates reaction mechanisms by constructing
candidate molecular configurations, locating and character-
izing stationary points, tracing elementary reaction paths,
and comparing competing channels under the specified re-
action conditions and computational requirements. A work-
flow graph organizes tool calls and computational tasks by
their execution dependencies. Cloudra’s Delivery DAG rep-
resents the mechanism being delivered: its nodes are species
occurrences, and its edges are elementary reaction steps.
Agents must express candidate species and their reaction
connections in this chemical graph. They choose subse-
quent geometric operations, calculations, and exploration
branches from their hypotheses and returned results.
Canonical topology and stereochemical comparisons
identify the species represented by a structure, while atom
mapping identifies corresponding atoms across reactant and
product configurations. Geometric operators rotate, trans-
late, and assemble molecular fragments. Frequency calcula-
tions determine stationary-point curvature, and IRC calcula-
tions followed by endpoint optimization and frequency anal-
ysis determine which species a saddle point connects.
The Coordinator proposes mechanistic hypotheses and
candidate elementary steps, and selects which branches to in-
vestigate. Computational agents prepare three-dimensional
structures with the geometric operators and submit quantum-
chemical calculations for their assigned steps. If a geometry
diverges, a saddle-point optimization fails to converge, or a
path bifurcates, the computational agent examines the struc-
tures and calculation diagnostics to decide whether to con-
tinue the step. If the step appears blocked, it reports the diag-
nosis to the Coordinator, which revises the hypothesis or ex-
plores another branch. The Supervisor reviews the species,
steps, and calculation results in the DAG against the task re-
quirements. Its review notes are stored separately from the
facts parsed from calculation outputs.
2.1.1 Asynchronous Computation Scheduling and
Resource Management
The calculation database ( ComputeDB) stores each job’s sta-
tus. Separate capacity pools limit concurrent calculations
on local workers, remote SSH clusters, and Slurm queues.
Before dispatch, the scheduler computes a SHA-256 digest
from the Cartesian coordinates, spin, charge, and level of the-
ory. The scheduler reuses a matching active job or cached
result. Operating-system exclusive file locks ( flock) track
running processes and concurrency; quantum-chemistry pro-
cesses holding active locks remain running during monitor
exceptions. When a calculation ends, a scheduler notifica-
tion triggers asynchronous output reading by the computa-
tional agent.

2.3 Chemical Species and Reaction Steps in the
Delivery DAG
2.3.1 Species Occurrences and Elementary Steps
The Delivery DAG is G = (V, E). Each vertex v ∈ V repre-
sents a species at one position in the reaction sequence, and
each directed edge e = ( vi, vj) ∈ E represents an elemen-
tary step. The mapping κ : V → S gives the canonical
chemical identity of each species occurrence. An initial and
a regenerated catalyst can therefore occupy separate nodes
while sharing the same chemical identity.
Each species is described by molecular geometry, bond-
ing topology, and stereochemistry. Its incoming and out-
going step edges describe its reaction connections to adja-
cent species. Canonical molecular graphs and stereochemi-
cal labels are compared when assigning structures to species;
atom mapping identifies corresponding atoms in reactant
and product structures. Species nodes record stationary-
point geometries, frequencies, and absolute energies; step

2.4 Calculation Diagnostics and Retries
The system distinguishes execution errors, numerical non-
convergence, and physical evidence problems. Execution
errors include invalid input syntax, environment faults, and
parsing failures. Numerical checks record self-consistent
field (SCF) and geometry optimization convergence. Physi-
cal evidence problems include saddles with nimag ̸= 1 , IRC
endpoints awaiting verification, or molecular graphs corre-
sponding to a different species. Imaginary-mode participa-
tion and geometric interval measurements indicate which
atomic motions or distances to inspect.
Let T contain the species and elementary steps requested
by the task. Extending Pe,k to each t ∈ T , the calculation-
stage requirements to be resolved are
U = {(t, k) : t ∈ T, k ∈ Kt, P t,k = 0}. (2.15)
For each (t, k) ∈ U , diagnostic feedback identifies the
affected species or step and the problem: SCF noncon-
vergence, normal-mode misalignment, a missing IRC-to-
endpoint calculation link, or a molecular graph mismatch.
After a failed calculation, the system retains its output, up-
dates U , and reports the geometry and convergence informa-
tion to the agent.
Calculations that depend on a failed stage are rescheduled;
earlier attempts and raw outputs are retained. The system
reorders atom indices, assigns each calculation result to its
species or step, and updates the graph status. The model re-
vises the chemical constraints, dihedral angles, or electronic-
structure method for the next attempt.
2.5 Implementation Defaults
The implementation described here corresponds to Cloudra
revision 70daa5a.[ 20] Numerical arrays and linear algebra
use NumPy[21]; quantum-chemical quantities are extracted
with cclib alongside engine-specific native-log readers[ 22];
and benchmark graph representation and matching use
NetworkX[23]. Preorganization uses 32 rigid orientations,
up to 8 guided placements, minimum interfragment separa-
tion 1.0 Å, fragment-contact multiplier 1.7, and heavy-atom
RMSD clustering threshold 0.5 Å. MMFF94 relaxation uses
up to 2000 iterations per phase with a distance restraint force
constant of 100.0. Standard IDPP employs 10 images opti-
mized via MDMin (100 steps, force tolerance fmax = 0 .1,
NEB spring constant k = 0.1); lightweight IDPP uses Adam
optimization (4000 steps, step size 0.02, gradient tolerance
10−7). Path convergence criteria evaluate the maximum
force residual on intermediate path images.
The mapping coeﬀicients and the Jmin + 2 ambiguity win-
dow are fixed ranking heuristics. Charge and radical mis-
matches carry larger costs than hydrogen-count and bond-
edit mismatches. Branch crowding uses the fixed decay
ρ = 0 .5, traversal through depth five, and congestion flag
Baxis ≥ 9.0.
Cloudra also provides a conformer-search tool with RD-
Kit and external CREST backends.[24] The optional CREST
backend samples conformers or noncovalent complexes, fol-
lowed by quantum-chemical optimization and physical vali-
dation.
2.6 CloudraBench construction and experimental
design
2.6.1 Task coverage and tier rationale
CloudraBench evaluates end-to-end mechanism calculations
on 50 tasks: 20 Easy tasks examine elementary transforma-
tions, conformational changes, and basic execution reliabil-
ity; 10 Normal tasks examine intermediate identification and
multistep continuity; 10 Hard tasks examine competing path-
ways and stereochemical identity; and 10 Impossible tasks
examine the limits of the allowed methods and justified neg-
ative conclusions (Figure 5). Related reactions can appear in
different tiers when they require different pathways, config-
urations, or electronic states. Tasks were selected using lit-
erature sources and reference calculations, considering both
their ability to distinguish workflow performance and their
computational cost. An accompanying catalogue lists each
task’s name, input conditions, and allowed methods.
2.6.2 Reference answers and evaluation protocol
Each task fixes the chemical conditions, charge, spin, de-
liverables, and allowed methods. Easy, Normal, and Hard
tasks permit xTB[ 25], PM7[ 26], and B3L YP-D3(BJ)/def2-
SVP[27–32]; Impossible tasks are restricted to xTB and
PM7. Computational evidence is read from native ORCA,
Gaussian, or MOPAC logs.[33–36] Reference answers spec-
ify species, elementary steps, stereochemical identities, and
applicable numerical or negative targets. Numerical barriers
are electronic energy differences at a consistent level of the-
ory; stable-minimum references and actual IRC connectivity
are verified separately. For each accessible reaction step re-
quiring verification, we use TS frequency analysis, bidirec-
tional IRCs, and endpoint optimization and frequency anal-
ysis. We also check that each endpoint optimization starts
from the actual terminal IRC frame. Species-property and
electronic-state questions are checked against their specified
targets.
2.6.3 Scoring and study configurations
Graph structure, self-consistency, and accuracy are evalu-
ated separately and combined with weights of 0.1, 0.3, and
0.6. Graded physical support and runtime delivery complete-
ness are also reported. A correctly identified method limita-
tion is scored as a supported negative conclusion. Each task
lists reference-graph availability, available reference calcu-
lations, task-specific exceptions, and unresolved validation
checks.

3 Results and Discussion
3.1 A two-step mechanism expressed through chemical
objects
The Guided NI1 run computes methylamine addition to
formaldehyde and carbinolamine dehydration to an imine–
water complex (Figure 6). Its Delivery DAG connects the
optimized reactant complex, carbinolamine, and product
complex through two first-order saddles. Calculations used
B3L YP-D3(BJ)/def2-SVP in the gas phase, with charge zero
and singlet multiplicity.
The addition saddle has one imaginary frequency at
−1481.06 cm−1 and an electronic barrier of 28.12 kcal/mol
relative to the reactant complex. Its two short IRC branches
lead, after endpoint optimization and frequency analysis,
to the reactant complex and carbinolamine, both with zero
imaginary frequencies. The dehydration saddle has one
imaginary frequency at −1796.84 cm−1 and an electronic
barrier of 57.21 kcal/mol relative to its IRC-side carbino-
lamine minimum. Its two branches similarly yield carbino-
lamine and the imine–water complex. Each IRC followed 24
points in each direction before reaching the point limit; the
endpoint calculations establish the resulting minima.
Interconversion between the carbinolamine conformers
remains uncomputed. Supplementary Information Sec-
tion S4 provides the conformer references, absolute energies,
native calculation records, and figure inputs.
3.2 End-to-end benchmark evaluation
CloudraBench measures end-to-end mechanism-calculation
capability: constructing candidate mechanisms, carrying
out the required quantum-chemical calculations, and reach-
ing task-consistent positive or negative conclusions. We
report composite scores together with their graph, self-
consistency, and accuracy components. Offline postprocess-
ing reconstructs mechanism graphs from each run’s own re-
search materials and compares the computed species, reac-
tion channels, electronic states, and barrier references with
the task definition. Before calculating Accuracy, we match
each reported reaction channel and electronic state to the tar-
get specified in the task. All four workflows use the same
postprocessing rules. The resulting 200-run table contains
all three components for every run, with an overall compos-
ite mean of 0.7176. Figures 7–11 report runtime graph pro-
duction, TS–IRC–endpoint evidence, and agreement with
the task’s answer.

3.2.2 Self-consistency contributes most to the
composite-score gain
The three components score different parts of the calcula-
tion (Figure 8). Graph measures the delivered coverage
and identities of the requested species and steps. Self-
consistency measures the support linking saddle points, fre-
quencies, IRC starts, and endpoint optimization/frequency
calculations. Accuracy measures agreement with the task’s
numerical or negative target.
Across all 50 Guided–Standard task pairs, the weighted
graph contribution to the mean improvement was +0.0108,
self-consistency contributed +0.1179, and accuracy
+0.0927, accounting together for the composite gain of
0.2214. For the 20 Normal and Hard tasks, the mean gain
was 0.2164, with contributions of +0.0080 from graph,
+0.1247 from self-consistency, and +0.0837 from accuracy.
Within the ten Normal tasks, the corresponding contribu-
tions were +0.0098, +0.1141, and +0.0400; within the ten
Hard tasks they were +0.0063, +0.1353, and +0.1274.
On Hard tasks, the weighted accuracy improvement is
comparable in size to the self-consistency improvement.
HH1 provides a direct example. Guided and Standard
both reported a binaphthyl racemization barrier of 23.48
kcal/mol, close to the 23.01 kcal/mol reference. Both had
graph scores of 1 and accuracy scores of approximately
0.973. Their self-consistency scores were 1.0 and 0.5, pro-
ducing composite scores of 0.9838 and 0.8338, respectively.
The archived Standard study contained candidate channels
and IRC inputs; the required endpoint connections remained
unverified. Guided attached the path evidence to the cor-
responding saddle and endpoints. HI1 shows a related pat-
tern: accuracy was approximately 1 in both runs, while self-
consistency increased from 0.2333 to 1.0 and the composite
score from 0.7032 to 0.9999.

3.3.1 Equivalent SMILES and answer order affect
molecular-identity judgments
A geometric modification first requires identifying the
molecule, atoms, and reaction center to which it applies. A
molecule can be represented by different SMILES traversals,
while a molecular formula can describe different connec-
tivities. To examine how the model handles these distinc-
tions, we constructed 16 molecular-identity pairs covering
identical identities, connectivity changes within the same
molecular formula, changes in connectivity and charge loca-
tion, double-bond stereochemistry, and protonation changes.
Each pair was presented using the original strings or equiva-
lent rewritings and with two answer orders, yielding 64 judg-
ments.
The model answered 58 correctly. Equivalent rewriting
changed the semantic judgment in 4 of 32 matched repre-
sentation comparisons; reversing answer order changed the
judgment in 6 of 32 matched option-order comparisons. All
six of the latter cases involved repeated selection of the let-
ter A. One C 5H8 pair in Figure 14 illustrates this behavior
directly: an endocyclic and an exocyclic double bond define
different connectivities, yet the model answered A under all
four conditions, although A meant “same” in one order and
“different” in the other.
Cloudra stores molecular connectivity separately from co-
ordinates and uses atom correspondence to identify which
atoms to measure, move, or compare. Product-endpoint
alignment, path interpolation, and reaction-center measure-
ments retain this correspondence as the structure’s coordi-
nates change.

and target geometry, then compares the measured distances
or angles of the resulting candidates. Assigning these numer-
ical operations to software follows the approach of program-
aided language models.[40]
3.3.3 Feedback must match the specified atoms and
geometric objective
In an actual E1 trajectory, the model sought to bring the dihe-
dral angle defined by the transferring hydrogen H7, C2, C3,
and leaving atom Cl4 close to 180°. It rotated the fragment
on the C3 side about the C2–C3 axis by +120° and −120°,
producing candidates A and B, and set the C3–Cl4 distance
to 2.35 Å. The representative dihedral in the default structure
report involved H5, whereas the current objective involved
H7. The model then explicitly measured H7–C2–C3–Cl4 in
both candidates, obtaining approximately 59.4° and 179.4°,
and used candidate B in its next actual submission.
Figure 17 shows Newman projections along C2 →C3
drawn directly from the saved candidate coordinates, and
Figure 18 summarizes the corresponding target-angle resid-
uals. Candidate B places the H7 target angle within 0.6° of
180°, compared with a residual of 120.6° for A.
The corresponding residuals for the H5 comparison were
124.2° and 115.8° in A and B, respectively. Identifying the
anti-periplanar H7–Cl4 arrangement in B required the H7-
based dihedral.
When local probes provided only H5 measurements, the
model requested an additional H7 measurement under all
three cyclic option permutations. Adding H7 measurements
led it to select B under all three permutations. T wo further
paired tests of information suﬀiciency also shifted from re-
questing additional measurements to judging the informa-
tion suﬀicient when the target quantity was added.

3.4 Identifying the species and pathways established by
calculations
3.4.1 Associating frequencies and IRC endpoints with
the same saddle
The frequency calculation and IRC must use the declared
saddle, and each endpoint optimization must start from the
corresponding IRC terminal frame, followed by frequency
analysis of the resulting minimum. The DAG links these cal-
culations to the species and step and records their execution,
convergence and physical checks.
We extracted four source evidence chains from the archive
and constructed versions that preserved the links or changed
a single reference. Crossing these with flat and linked rep-
resentations and two answer orders yielded 32 inputs. The
altered references concerned the frequency geometry, IRC
starting geometry, endpoint-optimization input, or target
species; local termination, convergence, energy, and fre-
quency values were held constant. Figure 23 illustrates the
E1 case: changing the frequency geometry reference from
the saddle point to an existing endpoint changes how that
frequency result supports the current step.
All 16 judgments with linked representations were correct,
compared with 14 of 16 with flat representations. Both er-
rors occurred when a reference had been changed and the
options reversed, in E1 and the second NB1 step. In subse-
quent native follow-up calls on two selected source chains,
enabling thinking yielded correct judgments for all 16 judg-
ments of the evidence relationships, including the two previ-
ously incorrect cases.
The model detected every altered reference in the linked
format, even though the local numerical values were un-
changed. This presentation effect is consistent with obser-
vations that graph encoding affects relational reasoning in
language models.[42] Cloudra links each species, step and
calculation attempt to its calculation results, showing which
saddle, IRC and endpoint calculations belong together.

3.4.2 Diagnostic feedback on IRC connectivity and
transition-state curvature
Diagnostic feedback connects an evidence defect to the
chemical object and calculation that require revision.
Cloudra’s structured diagnostics identify specific problems
in quantum-chemical outputs, including steric-clash atom
pairs, imaginary-mode misalignment, and disconnected tra-
jectory endpoints. The agent can then change the clashing
geometry, inspect the imaginary mode, or recalculate the af-
fected endpoint.
T wo archived trajectories illustrate this connection. In
a Brief-errors HA1 trial, compact error codes replaced the
detailed IRC-endpoint linkage diagnostics. The Compu-
tational Agent repeatedly substituted source-minimum ref-
erences while the geometric disconnection remained un-
resolved. In a Guided NB1 trial, the verification found
zero imaginary frequencies and returned a normal-mode
diagnostic. The Coordinator requested a retry with re-
vised transition-state guess parameters and optimization con-
straints, which the Computational Agent used in the resched-
uled calculation.[43]
3.4.3 IA1: a completion request with a missing IRC
calculation
An archived IA1 trajectory shows how a completion request
can conflict with the recorded scientific requirements. The
reaction step had mandatory bidirectional short_irc verifica-
tion pending, and the waiver list was empty. The Supervisor
had earlier commented positively on intermediate optimiza-
tion convergence. The model subsequently requested com-
pletion while the IRC requirement remained unresolved.
The harness rejected that request and returned the out-
standing short_irc requirement to the model. In the next sub-
mission, the model changed its status to partial completion
(Figure 25).
We also tested whether the model could identify the out-
standing IA1 requirements from the recorded state. All 12
judgments were correct (100%) across six pairs with re-
versed answer-option order, using both a minimal summary
and an extended 12-message history.
3.5 Workflow guidance, scientific judgment, and
outlook
Across the 50-task comparison, Guided had higher mean
self-consistency and accuracy than Standard. On Hard
tasks, their weighted contributions to the gain were +0.1353
and +0.1274. The HH1 and HI1 results show how IRC
and endpoint calculations establish the connections behind
reference-consistent barriers.
HA8 and NU6 show why energy references and saddle
assignments must follow the computed structures and paths.
In HA8, the stable reactant minimum defines the barrier ref-
erence, while the IRC connects the saddle to a local reactive
conformer. In NU6, guesses for betaine-to-product closure
repeatedly optimized toward the direct reactant-to-product
saddle region. The DAG keeps the reactant conformer, sad-
dle, IRC endpoints and assigned step together so that the
agent can check which structure each energy belongs to after
a new calculation. The calculations provide external feed-
back for the agent’s planning and verification.[44]
These cases call for different next steps: calculate a miss-
ing endpoint, revise a guess that returns to another route, or
reassess a method that cannot describe the requested elec-
tronic state. The agent must update its reaction description
when the computed structures or paths change.[ 45] Record
retained the tools and obtained lower self-consistency than
Guided, particularly on Normal and Hard tasks. The
archived trajectories show how tool feedback directs the next
geometric operation or calculation.[ 43, 46]
Brief-errors scored higher on IA1 and IA9, whereas
Guided scored higher on IG8. These crossed outcomes mo-
tivate a task-dependent hypothesis: detailed diagnostics may
help repair a TS or IRC calculation, with limited benefit from
further local repair when the method or target needs recon-
sideration.
In G2, repeated checks led the agent to delete numerical
values already supported by native calculations. Supervi-
sor feedback restored the fields before further rejection led
to their final deletion. A numerical observation and its log
should remain attached to the graph while its mechanistic
assignment is being tested.
P2 contains one run per task and configuration; its task-
cluster bootstrap intervals describe variation across tasks.
P1b provides three repeats on each of four tasks under each
configuration, totalling 48 runs. Some Hard-task Accuracy
targets score qualitative channel identity, so the reported
gain includes pathway identification and evidence comple-
tion as well as numerical barriers.
Standard sometimes ended research while calculations
were still running, whereas Guided/HI1 remained waiting af-
ter the completion notification. Returning results therefore
need to resume the unfinished study before final reporting.
NU6’s repeated convergence to one saddle region also moti-
vates comparing new candidates with previous attempts be-
fore choosing another guess.
